Find the source first

Before we sell you a drain, we figure out where the water comes from. Roof runoff, a neighbor’s grade, a high water table and a crushed pipe all look the same from the basement. A walk around the house in the rain, or a close look at the downspouts, usually tells the story.

The fix might be a simple regrade and swale, or it might be a full drain system. You’ll know which, and why, before work starts.

Stormwater rules

Water has to go somewhere legal. You generally can’t collect runoff and send it onto a neighbor’s property, and most municipalities have stormwater ordinances, under the state’s Act 167 program, that control where new drainage can discharge. In Philadelphia, the Water Department reviews stormwater on larger projects. We design around those rules so the fix doesn’t create a new problem.

What affects the price

  • Length and depth of drain lines
  • Pipe, stone and fabric
  • Where the water can legally discharge
  • Hardscape or landscaping in the way
  • Access and lawn restoration

Drainage questions

Why is my basement wet after it rains?

Most often it’s surface water: downspouts dumping next to the foundation, or ground that slopes toward the house. Fixing the grading and roof runoff solves a lot of wet basements before anything goes underground.

Can I pipe my downspouts to the street?

Sometimes. Many municipalities regulate curb outlets and storm sewer tie-ins, and some don’t allow them. We check the local rule and use an infiltration option where a street connection isn’t allowed.

What’s the difference between a French drain and a swale?

A swale is a shallow channel shaped into the surface to carry water across a yard. A French drain is a buried trench of stone and perforated pipe that collects groundwater and seepage. Many yards need a bit of both.
